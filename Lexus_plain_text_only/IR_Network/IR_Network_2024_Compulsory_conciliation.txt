IR - Editorial

                                            Compulsory conciliation                  

                                            Truworths                                             challenged the jurisdiction of the Labour Court to determine an automatically unfair dismissal dispute, alternatively an alleged unfair labour practice dispute in                                             that                                             the dispute                                             had not been referred to the CCMA for conciliation prior to approaching the                                             Labour                                             Court for adjudication.                    1        

                                            Although                                             not necessary for the decision on jurisdiction, Peter had a troubled time at Truworths. He was employed as TDC technician from August 2017 until he was dismissed in July 2022.                   

                                            Starting in March 2018 Peter and Truworths were in dispute about his claim that he was entitled to paid overtime. He referred a dispute to the CCMA and in October of that year it was resolved.                   

                                            About a year later, Peter referred a complaint to the Truworths                                             anonymous tip-off line, alleging that company assets had gone missing. Truworths responded and after an investigation, certain individual employees were disciplined. In December 2019 Peter submitted another tip-off, this time complaining that he had suffered v                                            ictimisation, harassment  and bullying particularly by a named manager and his ‘allies’. He went further, alleging that Truworths had shared his original tip-off                                             with certain implicated em                                            ployees who then attempted to ‘cover their tracks’.                                             Peter alleged that Truworths had failed to protect him as a whistleblower although he had made a protected disclosure.                   

                                            During 2020 and 2021                                             Peter made numerous allegations of sabotage in Truworths                                            ’ distribution centre. After extensive investigation Peter could not furnish cogent evidence to support a finding of sabotage a                                            n                                            d in April 2022 the investigation was closed.                   

                                            Sometime                                             in March 2022 Peter was injured on duty. He was examined by the company doctor and he consulted a private doctor. The company wanted the matter to be referred as a workers’ compensation claim (entirely unexceptional). Peter                                             protested. Over a period of three months of to and fro between Peter and his line manager he was uncooperative and claimed he was being harassed. Eventually in May 2022 he was accused of gross insubordination, insolence and breach of company policy and procedure. We don’t need to set ou                                            t the detail. He was given a final written warning, valid for 12 months. If he did not accept                                             this,                                             he would be entitled to defend himself in a disciplinary hearing. He did so and the warning was confirmed.                   

                                            In July 2022 the saga of the ID continued. Peter’s failure to cooperate is not explained. He was then suspended and brought before a hearing to answer to allegations of insolence, gross insubordination and incompatibility. At the disciplinary hearing the employee argued that he was bullied, threatened and discriminated against during the hearing                                            . He was found guilty of the allegations and dismissed on 20 July 2022.                   

                                            Peter referred an alleged unfair labour practice dispute to the CCMA alleging a                                             final written warning without prior warning and that the disciplinary hearing was procedurally unfair. The CCMA scheduled a con-arb process and during the conciliation phase the commissioner sought to clarify the nature of the dispute: an ULP and/or a dismissal dispute. Peter thought this was cross-questioning. The commissioner found the dispute was two                                            -fold: both an alleged ULP and                                             alleged constructive dismissal. It’s not clear what the constructive dismissal dispute was about                                             but that is                                             not relevant.                                             The commissioner                                             found that the referral was out of time; there was no condonation application. In any event there                                                                                         was no ULP and t                                            he claim was dismissed.                  

                                            The employee then approached the Labour Court making several allegations: failure to pay overtime, non-payment of salaries and creating a hostile, toxic and unbearable working environment in an attempt to force Peter to resign. He went back to his 2019 tip-offs contending these were protected disclosures in terms of the Protected Disclosures A                                            c                                            t                                             No. 26                                             of 2000 (PDA), that he had suffered an occupational detriment, making his dismissal automatically unfair.                                             Truworths disputed this                                            ,                                             arguing instead that the disclosures were not made in good fa                                            i                                            th                                             but related to the overtime dispute. The employer also argued that                                             the employee should have referred the alleged PDA contentions in terms of s 187 (h) of the LR                                            A                                             as an alleged automatically unfair                                             dismissal relating to alleged PDAs within the statutory time frame. He did not do so and the Labour Court                                             therefore                                             lacked jurisdiction.                                             The Court found                                             that                                             Peter was ‘unhelpful’. He said what he wanted, declin                                            ing                                             useful suggestions from the bench.                   

                                                                                                      “The dispute between the parties invokes two provisions of the LRA concerning protected disclosures, first being section 186 (2), which defines an unfair labour practice as when an employee suffers an occupational detriment (other than a dismissal) on account of having made a protected                                             disclosure                                             as                                             defined in the PDA                                            . In the second instance, section 187 (1) (h) provides that where an employee has been dismissed on account of having made a protected disclosure and thus the employer is in contravention of the PDA, such dismissal is automatically unfair.                  

                                                                                                      The PDA aims to                                             make                                             provision for procedures in ter                                            ms of which employees and workers may disclose information regarding wrongful or illegal conduct of their employer or their co-employees, and to provide for the protection or such whistleblowers against retaliation for the disclosures made.”                  

                                                                                                      …                  

                                                                                                      To qualify as a                                             protected                                             disclosure under section 6 of the PDA, such disclosure                                             must                                             be made to the disclo                                            ser                                            ’                                            s employer and must be made in good faith.                    2        

                                            The LAC reminded that in an automatically unfair dismissal dispute, a complainant has an election: to have the dispute determined in the Labour Court by a                                            djudication or by the CCMA by way of arbitration. However, in either instance an employee must refer the dispute to the CCMA for conciliation. This is a mandatory step and an employee may not bypass this process.                   

                                            The employee made much of                                             the fact that the commissioner or the employee, or both, characterised the dismissal as a constructive dismissal. “The label attached to dismissal referred to conciliation is of no consequence. The issue as to the kind of dismissal only becomes relevant at the time o                                            f                                             arbitration or adjudication, and if unclear, it might only become clear after all the evidence is presented at the arbitration or the adjudication.”                    3        

                                            Because no conciliation took place the dispute was not ripe for adjudication and he could only proceed, either in the Labour Court or in the CCMA after a conciliation process.                                             As it stood, the employee would have had to have the commissioner’s findings set aside on review and the matters properly conciliated as required by the LRA.                                             The LAC held that the Labour Court erred in dismissing the appellant’s special pleas. In the                                             result, the late filing of the appeal record was condoned, the appeal was upheld with no order as to costs.                   

                                            T                                            his is perhaps a case in which Peters would have been well served with some helpful                                            ,                                             firm advice                                            ,                                             But there are parties who are                                             less receptive to guidance                                            .
