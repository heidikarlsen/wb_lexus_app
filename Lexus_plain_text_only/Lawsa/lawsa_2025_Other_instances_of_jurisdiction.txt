The Law of South Africa (LAWSA)


LAWSA  >  The Law of South Africa (LAWSA)  >  Labour Law: Part 2  >  Resolution Of Labour Disputes  >  Adjudication Of Labour Disputes  >  Labour Court  >  Jurisdiction

267  Other instances of jurisdiction  The director of the Commission for Conciliation, Mediation and Arbitration (CCMA) must, after application by a party, refer a matter1 to the Labour Court in terms of section 191(6) if the director decides that it would be appropriate considering:
(a) the reason for dismissal;
(b) whether there are questions of law raised by the dispute;
(c) the complexity of the dispute;
(d) whether there are conflicting arbitration awards that need to be resolved; and
(e) the public interest.
  An employee may refer a dispute concerning an alleged unfair labour practice to the Labour Court for adjudication if the employee has alleged that he or she has been subjected to an occupational detriment by the employer in contravention of section 3 of the Protected Disclosures Act 2 for having made a protected disclosure defined in that Act.3
  The Labour Court has concurrent jurisdiction with the High Court 4 in respect of any alleged or threatened violation of any fundamental right entrenched in chapter 2 of the Constitution5 and arising from:6
(a) employment or from labour relations;7
(b) any dispute over the constitutionality of any executive or threatened executive act, administrative act or conduct by the state8 in its capacity as employer; and
(c) the application of any law for the administration of which the Minister of Labour 9 is responsible.
  If a labour-related matter is referred to arbitration in terms of the Arbitration Act,10 and that matter could have been arbitrated in terms of the Act, the Labour Court has jurisdiction to review such arbitration.11
  The Labour Court is granted jurisdiction to refuse to determine any dispute, other than an appeal or review, before the court, if the court is not satisfied that an attempt has been made to resolve the dispute through conciliation. The Labour Appeal Court confirmed that conciliation was a mandatory prerequisite for all disputes referred in terms of section 191(5) of the Labour Relations Act.12
  A certificate issued by a commissioner of the CCMA or a bargaining or statutory council stating that a dispute remains unresolved is sufficient proof that an attempt has been made to resolve that dispute through conciliation.13
  Once the employment relationship has been terminated, the Labour Court does not have jurisdiction to make an order to prohibit the erstwhile employer to take steps against the erstwhile employee.14 This does not mean that the Labour Court does not have jurisdiction to make an order in respect of a matter that happened whilst the employment relationship still existed or in terms of the contract of employment.
  The Labour Court does not have jurisdiction to deal with a delictual claim between a trade union and its members.15 The Labour Court also does not have jurisdiction if a dispute arises between shop stewards and the union if the shop stewards are not employed by the union.16
  A person may have more than one cause of action arising out of the same event but has to elect on what cause of action he or she would prefer to base his or her case. Once an election has been made as to the cause of action, that election determines whether the dispute would fall within the Labour Court’s jurisdiction.17
  The Labour Court would not enter into the adjudication of tax disputes, an arena properly regulated under tax legislation, nor would it make orders incidental to an investigation into the parties’ liability to pay income tax.18

In Fedlife Assurance v Wolfaardt 2001 ILJ 2407 (SCA) and Fredericks v MEC for Education & Training, Eastern Cape 2002 ILJ 81 (CC); 2002 2 BCLR 113 (CC) it was unanimously decided that the High Court had concurrent jurisdiction with the Labour Court to determine employment matters which arose from the common-law contract of employment, as well as from the alleged or threatened violation of fundamental rights under the Constitution arising from employment and labour relations, disputes over the constitutionality of any executive or administrative acts or conduct by the state in its capacity as an employer and disputes about the application of labour laws, as summarised in Nakin v MEC, Department of Education, Eastern Cape Province 2008 ILJ 1426 (E). The rationale is that s 169 of the Constitution gives certain functions to the High Court that cannot be overruled by the Labour Relations Act 66 of 1995. Therefore, in order to determine the Labour Court’s jurisdiction, one has to analyse the Labour Relations Act. There is no general jurisdiction given to the Labour Court, it only has the rights granted to it by the Act. The Labour Court also does not have exclusive jurisdiction in all labour matters between an employer and an employee. It depends on how a claim is formulated. The fact that a claim could also have been formulated as an unfair labour practice does not mean that, if a claim is not formulated as an unfair labour practice, the Labour Court has exclusive jurisdiction of a claim so formulated. An employee may therefore sue in the High Court for a dismissal based on a breach of contract or non-compliance with a disciplinary code that forms part of a contract of employment. See also par 44 ante. The debate which seemed to be settled law after the Fedlife case supra was opened again in Chirwa v Transnet 2008 ILJ 73 (CC). In this case, an employee of a parastatal was charged with poor performance and dismissed. Conciliation at the CCMA failed. The employee then approached the High Court on the basis that her dismissal violated her right to fair administrative action as enshrined in the Constitution of 1996. The Constitutional Court decided that the Labour Court had jurisdiction in this matter. They found that this was a dismissal and not an administrative law dispute and that the employee should have followed the procedure in the Labour Relations Act 66 of 1995. The differences in the Fedlife and Chirwa cases were again visited by the Constitutional Court in Gcaba v Minister of Safety & Security 2010 ILJ 296 (CC). Although the Labour Court is given concurrent jurisdiction with the High Court in s 157(2), the Constitutional Court made a policy decision that, where the Act determines that the Labour Court has jurisdiction, the High Court does not have concurrent jurisdiction, thereby giving s 157(1) an expansive content. See also Du Toit 2010 ILJ 21. It might be unlikely that the dual system created by the Supreme Court of Appeal in Boxer Superstores supra and discussed in Pretorius and Myburgh 2007 ILJ 2173 and Murray v Minister of Defence 2008 ILJ 1369 (SCA), will withstand constitutional scrutiny by the Constitutional Court, based on the principles laid down in the Gcaba case supra. See also SAMSA v McKenzie 2010 ILJ 529 (SCA).
