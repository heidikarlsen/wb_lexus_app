



Bobotjane / Sebvest Financial Services Group / Sebvest Development Africa (Pty) Ltd [2021] 1 BALR 7 (CCMA)
Arbitration Law Reports

Division: Commission for Conciliation, Mediation and Arbitration
Case No: GAEK7449-20
Date: 11/09/2020
Before: W Koekemoer, Commissioner
Keywords


Referral in terms of section 191(5)(a)(iv) of the LRA
Unfair labour practice — Suspension — Employee sent home during lockdown because employer did not require his services — Suspension not constituting unfair labour practice.

Editor’s Summary


After the lockdown to combat the Covid-19 pandemic, the applicant, who was employed by the respondent solely to drive the owner’s children to school, was told that his services were not required while schools were closed. The applicant claimed that he had been unfairly suspended and sought compensation equal to 12 months’ wages. The respondent claimed that due to the lockdown he had no money to pay the applicant and no work for him to perform.

The Commissioner noted that a suspension may constitute an unfair labour practice if it is of a disciplinary nature. In this case, the applicant had been suspended solely because there was no work for him to perform due to the lockdown. The applicant and the respondent were both in the same boat as many other South Africans due to the lockdown. He was free to claim UIF payments if he qualified or to refer an unfair dismissal dispute if he was not permitted to resume work when the lockdown ended.

The application was dismissed for want of jurisdiction.

Award

Details of hearing and representation
This matter was heard at the offices of the CCMA Tshwane on 1 September 2020. 
The applicant represented himself. The respondent was represented by Mr G Sebulela, the owner of the company.
The respondent handed up Annexure “R”. 
The applicant handed up Annexure “A”. 
The proceedings were digitally recorded. 
[2021] 1 BALR 7 (CCMA) at 8

Background to the dispute
The respondent employs the applicant as a Personal Driver mainly to transport his children from home to school and back. 
During the Covid-19 lockdown, the respondent suspended the services of the applicant due to the schools being closed. 
On 6 August 2020, the applicant referred an unfair labour practice dispute to this Commission. In his 7.11 referral document the applicant stated the following:
Length of service: “6 to 10 years”;
Gross Salary: “less than R18 000”;
Nature of the dispute: “Unfair Labour Practice”;
Summary of the facts: “Suspension without cause”;
Date dispute arose: “10 July 2020”;
Result required: “Compensation”.
The matter was set down on 1 September 2020 for con/arb. The matter could not be settled and a certificate of non-resolution was issued. The matter was arbitrated upon and concluded on the same date.

Issues to be decided 
Whether the suspension of the applicant amounted to an unfair labour practice relating to suspension in terms of section 186(2)(b) of the Labour Relations Act.
In the event of a finding that an unfair suspension culminated, the relief the applicant is entitled to, is to be determined.

Points in limine 
No points in limine were raised.

Survey of evidence and argument

The applicant’s case

The applicant, Mr Tennyson Bobotjane, confirmed as follows.

The respondent suspended him without any cause. 
He requested compensation to the value of twelve months of his salary. 
According to him he was not treated even-handedly. 
He was unnecessarily left in limbo before he received a letter of suspension. 

The respondent’s case

The respondent’s representative, Mr George Sebulela, confirmed as follows.

The applicant is in his employment mainly to transport his children from home to school and back.  
[2021] 1 BALR 7 (CCMA) at 9

  [18]   He had no other reasonable option but to suspend the applicant pending the Covid-19 pandemic. He confirmed that his children are still not back at school. 

Currently, he has no alternative work for the applicant. 
He did not treat the applicant unfairly or commit an unfair labour practice.
He is in the same position the applicant is. 
He is not in a financial position to pay the applicant. 

Argument

The applicant’s argument
The applicant argued as follows:
The respondent committed an unfair labour practice in suspending him. 
He requested to be compensated to the maximum. 

The respondent’s argument
The respondent’s representative argued as follows: 
The applicant finds himself in the same circumstances he and his company find themselves in. 
Covid-19 is the determining factor. 
He denies committing any unfair labour practice. 

Analysis of the evidence and argument
Onus of proof
It was required of the applicant to prove an unfair labour practice in that the respondent unfairly suspended him in terms of section 186(2)(b). 
It was common cause between the parties that the respondent suspended the applicant “as from 4 May 2020 till further notice”, which suspension was Covid-19 related and that the respondent did not suspend the applicant based on any intended or any disciplinary action against the applicant. 
In the matter of Special Investigating Unit v CCMA and others (JR5092014) [2017] ZALCJHB 127, it was held:
“[10] This matter is one where it can without any doubt, be concluded that the Commissioner went on a frolic of her own, misapplied and misinterpreted the law, exceeded her powers and essentially ‘felt sorry’ for the employee. To cap it, the Commissioner went a step further by granting Lubita what Counsel for the applicant correctly referred to ‘sympathy compensation’. The award cannot by any account pass the standard of a reasonable decision maker test. My conclusions in this regard are based on the following. 
[2021] 1 BALR 7 (CCMA) at 10
[11] An unfair labour practice in section 186(2) of the LRA is defined as:

‘Unfair labour practice’ means an unfair act or omission that arises between an employer and an employee involving:

(a) unfair conduct by the employer relating to the promotion, demotion, probation (excluding disputes about dismissals for a reason relating to probation) or training of an employee or relating to the provision of benefits to an employee;
(b) the unfair suspension of an employee or any other unfair disciplinary action short of dismissal in respect of an employee;
a failure or refusal by an employer to reinstate or re-employ a former(c) employee in terms of any agreement; and
an occupational detriment, other than dismissal, in contravention of the Protected Disclosures Act, 2000 (d)(Act No. 26 of 2000), on account of the employee having made a protected disclosure defined in that Act.’

. . .

[14] John Grogan, Employment Rights (1ed) (Juta & Co, Cape Town 2013) at 135-136, in analysing the provisions of section 186(2)(b) stated that:

‘To fall within the terms of section 186(2)(b), disciplinary action against an employee short of a dismissal must be disciplinary both in nature and in intent. Action is ‘disciplinary’ if it is aimed at correcting errant behaviour for which the employee is responsible. So, for example, a counselling session or a warning for incapacity does not fall within the scope of the definition. The definition is also concerned with disciplinary action. The decision to hold a disciplinary enquiry does not fall within the definition of an unfair labour practice- the action must have been instituted before an employee can refer a dispute relating to disciplinary action short of dismissal. The word action also suggests that employees may not refer a dispute over the content of an employer’s disciplinary policy. A dispute may be entertained only if the employer actually takes action. Only the Labour Court or, perhaps, the High Court, has the power to interdict a disciplinary hearing.’

[15] In this case therefore, the mere fact that an employee is aggrieved by the lodging of a complaint or the institution of some form of investigation against him or her cannot by any stretch of imagination be construed as disciplinary action, let alone one that is short of being a dismissal. An investigation or a complaint against an employee cannot be equated with disciplinary action. To hold otherwise would lead to absurdity and untenable consequences in that employers would ordinarily be reluctant to investigate any allegations of misconduct against employees, and employees in turn would be reluctant to lodge complaints against each other or their managers. The interpretation or application of section 186(2)(b) of the LRA cannot be stretched that far, and the Commissioner clearly misinterpreted those provisions.
[16] To the extent that there was no disciplinary action, let alone any action short of a dismissal, the Commissioner equally had no jurisdiction over the matter as there was no dispute between the parties. 

. . .

[18] . . . It is not for Commissioners to ‘feel sorry’ for employees and award them ‘sympathy compensation’ simply on the basis that they may have 

been inconvenienced by the employer’s conduct. Central to a determination of disputes under the provisions of section 186 of the LRA is whether the conduct of the employer was unfair. Thus, the facts of a case and the law determines an outcome, and not the Commissioner’s own sentiments.

. . .

[20] In conclusion therefore, the award issued by the Commissioner is reviewable on the grounds that the decision reached therein does not fall within a range of reasonableness in the light of the material placed before her. Essentially, the Commissioner lacked jurisdiction to determine the matter before her as there was no live dispute between the parties within the meaning of section 186(2)(b).” 
It was clear to me that the applicant is in the same shoes as many of his fellow-countrymen during the Covid-19 lockdown are. 
It was also clear to me that the applicant is in the same shoes as his own employer.
For the best part of four to five months there was no work and no income, even currently for his employer.
I could not find any special protection for the applicant against the dire Covid-19 consequences under the auspices of the unfair labour practice jurisdiction in terms of the Labour Relations Act. 
It is, however, to be emphasised that nothing stands in the way of the applicant to approach the Department of Labour pertaining to his outstanding UIF payments. 
Furthermore, the applicant is still an employee of the respondent and the Covid-19 lockdown should end soon.
In the event that the suspension of the applicant remains in force, despite the Covid-19 lockdown being lifted, the applicant could place his employer on terms and if his suspension is not uplifted, the applicant could consider referring an unfair dismissal dispute to this Commission.
See Special Investigating Unit v CCMA and others (JR5092014) [2017] ZALCJHB 127.

Award
In short, I find that the CCMA lacks jurisdiction to determine the matter, as there is no actual dispute between the parties within the meaning of section 186(2)(b) of the Labour Relations Act. 
The case of the applicant is herewith dismissed.
