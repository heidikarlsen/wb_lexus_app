



Jordan v MEC for Finance, Eastern Cape & another [2007] JOL 19802 (Ck)
Judgments Online

Division: Bisho 
Case No: 315 / 07 
Hearing Date(s): 16 / 04 / 2007 
Judgment Date(s): 17 / 04 / 2007 
Bench: PC van der Byl AJ 


Keywords


Civil procedure — Interdictory relief — Requirements — Prima facie right

Mini Summary


The applicant sought an order interdicting and restraining the respondents from commencing and proceeding with any disciplinary enquiry against him pending clarification of the Labour Court's reasons for judgment and the final determination of an unfair labour practice dispute referred to the bargaining council.

The Labour Court had granted the applicant an interim order interdicting and restraining the respondents from holding any disciplinary enquiry against the applicant pending finalisation of an unfair labour practice dispute to be lodged within 10 days of the granting of the order. 

Held that the applicant had failed to make out a case for the relief sought. Primarily, he could not satisfy the court that he had a prima facie right to the interdictory relief. The court fund that the respondents were not acting unlawfully in proceeding with disciplinary proceedings against he applicant, and dismissed the application.

 
[2007] JOL 19802 (Ck) at 1




Introduction

[1]    The applicant, Theodore Mncedisi Jordan, employed as general manager: physical asset management by the Department of Finance of the Eastern Cape Province, seeks, as a matter of urgency, an interim order interdicting and restraining the respondents, ie the MEC for Finance of the Eastern Cape and the Superintendent-General of the Department of Finance of the Eastern Cape, from commencing and proceeding with any disciplinary enquiry against him on 17, 18 and 19 April 2007, pending: 
[2007] JOL 19802 (Ck) at 2
(a) clarification of the Labour Court's reasons for judgment delivered on 4 April 2007; and

the determination of an unfair labour practice dispute submitted to the General Public Service Sectoral Bargaining Council on 21 February 2007 in terms of section 186(2)(d) of the Labour Relations Act 66 of 1995.

Relevant facts of the matter

[2]    On 8 February 2007 the Labour Court granted the applicant an interim order interdicting and restraining the respondents from holding any disciplinary enquiry against him pending: 

". . . finalisation of an unfair labour practice dispute to be lodged within ten (10) days of the granting of (that) order in terms of s 186(2)(d) of the Labour Relations Act 66 of 1996 (sic)."

Section 186(2)(d) (sic) of the Labour Relations Act 66 of 1995, merely defines an "unfair labour practice" as "an occupational detriment, other than dismissal, in contravention of the Protected Disclosures Act, 2000 (Act 26 of 2000), on account of the employee having made a protected disclosure defined in that Act".

I assume that the order was in effect intended to interdict the respondents from instituting any disciplinary proceedings against the applicant pending the finalisation of an "unfair labour practice dispute" to be referred by the applicant to the General Public Service Sectoral Bargaining Council in terms of section 191 of the Labour Relations Act 66 of 1995.

 
[2007] JOL 19802 (Ck) at 3

[3]    It seems to be common cause that the applicant, thereupon, indeed referred such a dispute to the General Public Service Sectoral Bargaining Council on 21 February 2007 and that such dispute is currently still pending.

The contents and nature of that dispute are, however, not disclosed in the papers.

If I understood the submissions made in argument correctly, it would seem that the respondents intend to conduct a disciplinary hearing against the applicant for misconduct on the grounds of certain allegations or disclosures he made in relation to the respondents or officers employed in the Department of Finance of the Eastern Cape Province, but that it is the applicant's contention or defence that those allegations or disclosures constitute "protected disclosures" as envisaged in section 1 of the Protected Disclosures Act 26 of 2000.

In terms of section 3 of that Act no employee may be subjected to any occupational detriment by his or her employer on account, or partly on account, of having made a "protected disclosure" as defined in section 1 of that Act.

An "occupational detriment" is defined in section 1 of that Act as, inter alia, being subjected to any disciplinary action.

In terms of section 4 of that Act any employee who has been subjected, is subject or may be subjected, to an occupational detriment in breach of section 3, may approach any court having jurisdiction, including the Labour Court for appropriate relief or pursue any other process allowed or prescribed by any law.

[4]    The order granted on 8 February 2007 was, however, discharged by the Labour Court (Pillay J) on 29 March 2007.

 
[2007] JOL 19802 (Ck) at 4

[5]    On 4 April 2007 the learned Judge indicated that she discharged the order for the following reasons, namely:

that the applicant has an appropriate alternative remedy, namely, to claim for an unfair labour practice in terms of section 186(2) of the Labour Relations Act 66 of 1995;
that the determination as to whether a disclosure is protected has to be determined by way of trial proceedings for the applicant has to prove, inter alia, that the disclosure he made was in good faith and that he has reasons to believe that the information he disclosed concerns acts of impropriety such as those listed in section 1(i) of the definition of "disclosure" in the Protected Disclosures Act 26 of 2000;
that there are disputes of fact which cannot be resolved by way of motion proceedings;
that an employer who persists with disciplinary action against an employee who has made disclosures runs the risk of prima facie proof being established that the employee was subjected to an occupational determination and that the employer bears the onus of proving that the proposed disciplinary action against the employee does not amount to an occupational detriment;
that no cause of action for unfair labour practices arises unless and until the employee is subjected to an occupational determination;
that investigations into the applicant's disclosure are ongoing and he is assisting with the investigations which assistance will not impair the investigations; and 
[2007] JOL 19802 (Ck) at 5
(g) that it is not appropriate to refer the application for oral evidence on the same papers:
firstly because the dispute has been referred for conciliation which has not yet taken place;
secondly, that the relief claimed is not to declare the disclosures to be protected but to interdict the disciplinary enquiry and to review the decision to refuse legal representation for the applicant during the disciplinary enquiry; and
thirdly, the court is opposed to hearing piecemeal applications to review interlocutory rulings made by disciplinary tribunals.

[6]    In view thereof that no particulars of the allegations contained in the application lodged in the Labour Court are disclosed in this application or that the papers in that application have not been made available to this Court, it is extremely difficult to understand these reasons in their proper context, but it would seem that the learned Judge discharged the order, inter alia, on the grounds thereof that the applicant failed to establish all the requirements for an interim or final interdict.

[7]    On 4 April 2007 the respondents served the applicant with a notice to attend a so-called "Reconvened Disciplinary Enquiry" on charges of misconduct to be held during the period 17 to 19 April 2007.

[8]    In a document purportedly filed in this Court the applicant seems to request the Labour Court to "urgently clarify Judge Pillay's reasons for judgment".

 
[2007] JOL 19802 (Ck) at 6

Although it is not clear why this document purports to have been filed in this Court, it would appear that the applicant filed this document because of a difference of interpretation between the applicant and the respondents' legal representative on the meaning and effect of the reasons furnished by Pillay J for the dismissal of the order granted on 8 February 2007.

On the one hand it would seem to be the applicant's interpretation of the reasons that the order was discharged because the applicant has an alternative remedy by way of the unfair labour dispute lodge with the General Public Service Sectoral Bargaining Council.

On the other hand, however, it would seem to be the interpretation of respondents' legal representatives that by having discharged the order the judge in effect held that the disciplinary proceedings can proceed.

[9]    Whatever, it is the aim of this application to interdict the respondents to proceed with the disciplinary proceedings against the applicant pending, as I have already indicated:

the determination of the unfair labour dispute lodged with the General Public Service Sectoral Bargaining Council in terms of section 191 of the Labour Relations Act 66 of 1995; and
the clarification of the Labour Court's aforesaid reasons. 
[2007] JOL 19802 (Ck) at 7

Respondents' submissions

[10]    It is the respondents' case that the application should in effect for at least three reasons be dismissed with costs, namely:

firstly, that this Court has no jurisdiction to hear this application; 
secondly, that, in so far as the applicant seeks an order interdicting the respondents from proceeding with the disciplinary enquiry against him, pending the finalisation or determination of the unfair labour dispute referred to the General Public Service Sectoral Bargaining Council, the Labour Court had in discharging the order granted on 8 February 2007 already decided that issue so that the issue is now in effect res judicata between the parties; and
thirdly, that the applicant in any event failed to establish the requirements for interim relief.

[11]    I deal seriatim with each of these contentions.

Jurisdiction

[12]    In terms of section 157 of the Labour Relations Act 66 of 1995, the Labour Court has exclusive jurisdiction in respect of all matters that elsewhere in terms of this Act or in terms of any other law are to be determined by the Labour Court.

[13]    As is apparent from the provisions of section 4 of the Protected Disclosures Act 26 of 2000, a matter pertaining to an occupational detriment or an allegation of occupational detriment is not limited to the exclusive jurisdiction of the Labour Court, and I am accordingly prepared to entertain this application in this Court.

 
[2007] JOL 19802 (Ck) at 8

The res judicata issue

[14]    This brings me to the difference of interpretation of the reasons furnished by Pillay J for having discharged the order made on 8 February 2007.

[15]    In my view, the applicant is wrong in so far as he contends that Pillay J held that the order be discharged because he has the alternative remedy referred to in the order dated 8 February 2007.

[16]    It is apparent that she in fact refused to grant a final interdict interdicting the respondents from proceeding with any disciplinary enquiry against the applicant notwithstanding the pending the unfair labour practice dispute referred to the General Public Service Sectoral Bargaining Council in terms of section 191 of the Labour Relations Act 66 of 1995, on the grounds thereof, as I have already indicated, that he failed to establish the requirements of a final interdict, particularly, that he has no other alternative remedy.

[17]    In having discharged the order in question the respondents are no longer debarred by way of an interdict from proceeding with the intended disciplinary proceedings.

[18]    This issue has accordingly been finally decided by the Labour Court and I, not sitting as a court of appeal on this issue, have no jurisdiction to decide that issue afresh.

[19]    It would seem to me that the applicant is in any event free to raise, as an alternative remedy, his defence of occupational detriment before the disciplinary tribunal and, may, should it be held against him, approach this Court or the Labour  
[2007] JOL 19802 (Ck) at 9
Court in terms of section 4 of the Protected Disclosures Act 26 of 2000, as has, as I understand the reasons of Pillay J for the discharge of the order granted on 8 February 2007, in fact been held by Pillay J.

Establishment of requirements of an interim interdict

[20]    It is trite that an applicant seeking an interim interdict is required to establish four requirements, namely, a prima facie right; a well-grounded apprehension of irreparable harm if an interim interdict is not granted and the ultimate relief is eventually granted; a balance of convenience in favour of the granting of an interim interdict; and the absence of any other satisfactory remedy.

[21]    In my view, the applicant in any event failed to establish any of these requirements.

[22]    In relation to the establishment of a prima facie right, the applicant in effect relies on the contention that the respondents are in law debarred from proceeding with any disciplinary proceedings against him:

pending the determination or finalisation of the unfair labour practice dispute referred to in terms of section 191 of the Labour Relations Act 66 of 1995; and
pending clarification of the Labour Court's reasons for discharging the order granted on 8 February 2007.

[23]    In relation to the first of these contentions, I have already indicated that the issue has already been decided by the Labour Court and that I, not sitting as a court appeal, cannot pronounce on the correctness or otherwise of that decision, but that it  
[2007] JOL 19802 (Ck) at 10
would seem that the applicant is in any event free to raise a defence of occupational detriment in the disciplinary proceedings. There is as far as I could have ascertained no rule (and my attention was not directed to any rule) that such a defence ought first to be determined or established by way of an unfair labour dispute in terms of section 186(2) of the Labour Relations Act 66 of 1995.

[24]    In relation to the second of these contentions, I know of no rule in terms of which "clarification" can be sought of reasons given by a judge of the Labour Court.

[25]    In my view, the respondents are not, prima facie or otherwise, acting unlawfully in proceeding with disciplinary proceedings against the applicant notwithstanding the fact that there is an unfair labour practice dispute pending in the General Public Service Sectoral Bargaining Council.

[26]    As far as the other requirements for an interim order are concerned, I cannot see how the applicant will suffer any irreparable harm if an interim order is not granted and he is bound to subject himself to the intended disciplinary proceedings subject, of course, to his right to raise a defence that the allegations or disclosures which are the subject-matter of the charge of misconduct are protected disclosures, and that the balance of convenience favours the granting of the interim order claimed.

[27]    For these reasons the applicant's application is dismissed with costs.
