The Law of South Africa (LAWSA)


LAWSA  >  The Law of South Africa (LAWSA)  >  Labour Law: Part 2  >  Public Service Employment  >  Framework And Principles  >  Perspectives On The Application Of Administrative Law In The Public Employment Relationship  >  Gcaba V Minister Of Safety And Security: Chirwa V Transnet Clarified And Qualified

368  Introduction  Given the uncertainty and divergence of positions that followed the decision of the court in Chirwa v Transnet,1 the Constitutional Court in Gcaba v Minister of Safety and Security 2 attempted to bring clarity and some direction to the debate.3 In this matter dealing with the failure to promote an employee, the court was asked to review and set aside a decision by the South African Police Service not to appoint an employee as a station commander. It held that Chirwa did not overrule Fredericks v MEC for Education and Training, Eastern Cape,4 but distinguished between the two cases.5 The court indicated that it would be useful to try to identify a few general principles and policy considerations which informed and have been informed by the interpretations put forward in Fedlife Assurance v Wolfaardt,6 Fredericks, Chirwa and other cases.7 Six such principles and considerations were relevant according to the court:
(a) The same conduct may threaten or violate different constitutional rights and give rise to different causes of action in law – often even to be pursued in different courts or fora. For example, aggressive conduct of a sexual nature in the workplace could constitute a criminal offence, violate equality legislation, breach a contract, give rise to a delictual action,8 and amount to an unfair labour practice. Rigid compartmentalisation should be avoided, as areas of law are labelled or named for purposes of systematic understanding and not necessarily on the basis of fundamental reasons for a separation.9
(b) Furthermore, the constitutional and legal order is one coherent system for the protection of rights and the resolution of disputes and it is generally accepted that human rights are intrinsically interdependent, indivisible and inseparable.10
(c) The court further stressed that legislation must not be interpreted to exclude or unduly limit remedies for the enforcement of constitutional rights.11
(d) However, the Constitution recognises the need for specificity and specialisation in a modern and complex society under the rule of law. The legislature is sometimes specifically mandated to create detailed legislation for a particular area of law, like equality, just administrative action (Promotion of Administrative Justice Act (PAJA))12 and labour relations (Labour Relations Act (LRA)):13 “Once a set of carefully-crafted rules and structures has been created for the effective and speedy resolution of disputes and protection of rights in a particular area of law, it is preferable to use that particular system. This was emphasised in Chirwa by both Skweyiya J and Ngcobo J. If litigants are at liberty to relegate the finely-tuned dispute resolution structures created by the LRA, a dual system of law could fester in cases of dismissal of employees.”14
(e) Therefore, forum shopping by litigants is not desirable. Once a litigant has chosen a particular cause of action and system of remedies (for example, the structures provided for by the LRA) he or she should not be allowed to abandon that cause as soon as a negative decision or event is encountered. In fact, the court emphasised, one may especially not want litigants to “relegate” the LRA dispensation because they do not “trust” its structures to do justice as much as the High Court could be trusted. After all, the court reminded, the LRA structures were created for the very purpose of dealing with labour matters.15
  (f )   Finally, the court stressed, it is useful to keep in mind the stare decisis principle, in terms of which, in the interests of certainty, equality before the law and the satisfaction of expectations, a court is bound by the previous decisions of a higher court and by its own previous decisions in similar matters.16
  In addressing the question whether the failure to promote and appoint the applicant constituted administrative action subject to review, the court made it clear that a twofold, but interrelated approach had to be adopted. First, it had to be determined whether the action complained of amounted to administrative action. This is necessary to determine the second question, namely, whether a particular institution enjoyed jurisdiction to hear the matter.17 Regarding the first question, it has to be borne in mind that, generally, employment and labour relationship issues do not amount to administrative action within the meaning of PAJA. This also appears from the constitutional distinction between fundamental labour rights18 and the right to just administrative action.19 Section 23 regulates the employment relationship between employer and employee and guarantees the right to fair labour practices. On the other hand, the ordinary thrust of section 33 is to deal with the relationship between the state as bureaucracy and citizens and guarantees the right to lawful, reasonable and procedurally fair administrative action. According to the court, section 33 does not regulate the relationship between the state as employer and its workers. Therefore, when a grievance is raised by an employee relating to the conduct of the state as employer and it has few or no direct implications or consequences for other citizens, it does not constitute administrative action.20
  In view of this, and with reference to the majority decision in Chirwa to the effect that the dismissal of the applicant in that case did not constitute administrative action, the court in Gcaba concluded that the failure to promote and appoint the applicant was not administrative action. Yet, the court emphasised that this would not always be the case: “The situation might be different where, for example, the appointment or dismissal of the National Commissioner of the SAPS is at stake. This decision is taken by the President as head of the national executive and is of huge public import.”21 Generally, however, whether the employer is regarded as private or public does not answer the question whether the conduct complained of is administrative action or an unfair labour practice. The nature of the matter appears to be decisive – in casu the issue is a quintessential labour-related issue. Furthermore, its impact is felt mainly by the applicant, and has little or no direct consequence for any other citizens. This, the court held, does not contradict the unanimous judgment in Fredericks.22 Therefore, if the applicant’s case proceeded in the High Court, he would have been destined to fail for not making out the case brought to the court, namely an application to review what he regarded as administrative action.23
  The finding that employment-related matters, also those emanating from the public employment sphere, does not generally constitute administrative action,24 has a substantial impact on the issue of duplicate jurisdiction and forum-shopping. The Labour Court and other LRA structures have been created as a special mechanism to adjudicate labour disputes such as alleged unfair dismissals grounded in the LRA and not, for example, applications for administrative review.25 This is confirmed by section 157(1) of the LRA, which grants the Labour Court exclusive jurisdiction over any matter that the LRA prescribes should be determined by it.26 Therefore, according to the Constitutional Court, section 157(1) should be given expansive content to protect the special status of the Labour Court, and section 157(2) should not be read to permit the High Court to have jurisdiction over these matters as well.27 The purpose of the latter provision28 is to extend the jurisdiction of the Labour Court to disputes concerning the alleged violation of any right entrenched in the bill of rights which arise from employment and labour relations, rather than to restrict or extend the jurisdiction of the High Court. In doing so, section 157(2) enhances the ability of the Labour Court to perform its role as a specialist court that is charged with the responsibility to develop a coherent and evolving employment and labour relations jurisprudence.29 Therefore, section 157(2) should not be understood to extend the jurisdiction of the High Court to determine issues which section 157(1) has expressly allocated to the Labour Court. Rather, section 157(2) implies that the Labour Court will be able to determine constitutional issues which arise before it, in the specific jurisdictional areas which have been created for it by the LRA, and which are covered by section 157(2).30
  However, as the court in Gcaba made clear, the LRA does not intend to destroy causes of action or remedies and section 157 should not be interpreted to do so. Where a remedy lies in the High Court (or, for that matter, in another court, such as the Equality Court), section 157(2) cannot be read to mean that it no longer lies there and should not be read to mean as much. This (also) flows from the fact that the Labour Court is a creature of statute with only selected remedies and powers, and does not have the power to deal with the common law or other statutory remedies. To hold otherwise, would have the effect of wiping out remedies.31
  Finally, the Constitutional Court agreed that, where the jurisdiction of a court is challenged, the matter must be resolved on the basis of the pleadings, and not on the substantive merits of the case. However, if the pleadings, properly interpreted, established that the applicant was asserting a claim under the LRA, the High Court would lack jurisdiction. Mr Gcaba’s application failed because his case was in reality an unfair labour practice claim – the High Court had therefore correctly ruled that he should have approached the Labour Court.32
  It is, therefore, evident that employment-related matters ascribed to the ( jurisdiction of the) Labour Court, and to other dispute resolution mechanisms provided for in the LRA, such as the Commission for Conciliation, Mediation and Arbitration (CCMA), do not constitute administrative action and can, therefore, not be entertained by a High Court on the basis of its administrative law and inherent jurisdiction. However, the question remains whether and, if so, to what extent the scope for the application of administrative law and, for that matter, of other areas of law, relevant to the public employment relationship exists.
  A related question concerns the jurisdiction of the appropriate institutions to deal with such matters. The following may be of relevance in this regard:
(a) There is a range of public employees who are not covered within the LRA sphere – in particular, members of the Defence Force and employees of the State Security Agency.33 While they may be able to rely in appropriate circumstances on, for example, contractual protection, they are also able to rely directly on the various constitutional fundamental rights, to the extent that sector-specific legislation does not provide protection.34 For them the consideration that the LRA, and for that matter the other labour laws, has created a specialist (labour law) dispensation does not arise. It is, therefore, suggested that in their case administrative law considerations remain relevant.
(b) Certain persons may for purposes of the issue at hand not be regarded as employees and may, therefore, not be able to claim protection under the existing labour laws. Two such instances include external applicants for positions within the public service,35 and certain public service pension fund-related matters (not necessarily involving the state as employer).36 For them also, if the nature of the conduct which is the subject of the claim, corresponds with the notion of an administrative act or decision, administrative law remains relevant.
(c) As the Labour Court, as is the case with the other LRA dispute-resolution institutions, is a creature of statute with only selected remedies and powers, it follows that there may be a range of issues which do not fall within the protective and regulatory sphere of the LRA and the other labour laws of South Africa.37 Within the public employment sphere, this potentially leaves scope for the application of administrative law.
(d) As noted by the court in Gcaba, there may be instances where the appointment or dismissal of certain (senior) public employees is at stake and of huge public import.38 This would be the case in particular where the position of the affected employee has a strong constitutional link, and where the appointment or dismissal has an obvious relationship to the principle of legality. It would appear that the decision in Majake v Commission for Gender Equality39 provides some support for this. In this matter, the summary dismissal of the chief executive officer of the Commission for Gender Equality was found to constitute a gross manifestation of arbitrary conduct which negated the Commission’s obligation and duty as a chapter 9 (of the Constitution) institution to act and conduct itself within the purview of legality. The termination was found to be inconsistent with the Constitution and the principles of the rule of law and therefore invalid.40 Also, in Nsele v National Commissioner of the SA Police Service41 the court held that the withdrawal by the National Commissioner of Police of all promotions in the province concerned and the re-advertising of the relevant positions constituted administrative action. This was far removed from the employer-employee relationship, as it affected not only the particular applicant, but also other candidates, and involved policy considerations.
(e) The Labour Court is also given the specific power to review any decision taken or any act performed by the state in its capacity as employer, on such grounds as are permissible in law.42 From this it follows that where, for example, the state as employer intends challenging the sanction imposed by a disciplinary hearing chairperson, it may, and according to the Supreme Court of Appeal in Ntshangase v MEC for Finance: KZN,43 may have to institute administrative law review proceedings to have the sanction set aside. The option to institute review proceedings under this provision is of course also available to public employees. It remains to be seen whether and, if so, to what extent the court will apply administrative law considerations when adjudicating the matter.44
  (f )   As noted above,45 with reference to the Supreme Court of Appeal judgment in Transman v Dick,46 the fact that public employees can no longer claim reliance on an administrative action to challenge the validity of dismissals, does not mean that parties cannot incorporate, expressly or impliedly, administrative law requirements into their employment agreements. The failure to comply with such requirements would, however, be a breach of contract and ordinary contractual claims would be available to the aggrieved party: “[T]he incorporated requirements cannot convert what is essentially a contractual claim into an entitlement to judicial review on any of the grounds recognised in law.”47 Therefore, in the public sector employment relationship, causes of action originating in contract and contractual remedies are still applicable and would vest jurisdiction in the High Court to intervene.
(g) As the Constitutional Court in Gcaba v Minister of Safety & Security48 emphasised, section 157 of the LRA should not be interpreted to imply that causes of action or remedies are destroyed. It is possible that a remedy may lie in the High Court or another court on a different basis, but with reference to the same employment-related factual context 49 – for both employees and other categories of persons (for example, office-bearers).50 As far as employees are concerned, for example, as indicated above,51 in a range of decisions concerning public employment the courts upheld the availability of a contractual claim, despite the availability of a claim (and remedy) based on the LRA or any of the other South African labour laws.52
(h) It needs to be remarked that it remains possible to approach the High Court on the basis of the general and well-recognised principles of legality, rationality and reasonableness.53
  Generally, however, post-Gcaba decisions tend to apply Chirwa and Gcaba, leading to an unwillingness to entertain labour-related disputes if a cause of action lies within the sphere of the Labour Relations Act.54
  Nevertheless, the effect of the approach adopted in Chirwa and, in particular, in Gcaba have been criticised given that it results in the fallacious position that the existence of a “primary” or, more specifically, applicable right (in Chirwa and Gcaba’s cases, the right to fair labour practices in section 23 of the Constitution) operates to the exclusion of the application of other, more general rights (namely, the right to just administrative action in section 33 of the Constitution).55 Hoexter has cogently explained the flaws in such an approach, which runs counter to the principle that litigants ought to be entitled to rely on the full protection of any and all applicable rights. The contrary position would result in more “general” constitutional rights, such as the right to just administrative action being completely negated in matters involving other (more specific) constitutional rights, such as environmental and property claims.56
  Similarly, as has been noted, Loots argues that both administrative and labour law are aimed at the promotion of social justice and that “even though different rights are expressed in separate provisions of the Bill of Rights, the normative web of the Constitution dictates against an interpretation that views fundamental rights as forever unconnected regardless of the circumstances of a case”.57 Loots’s thesis highlights that the rights of fair labour practices and just administrative action, which are informed by the “living norm” of fairness, are not isolated in function and must be interpreted, adapted and applied in a flexible manner and with due regard to contemporary political, social and economic contextual considerations.58 Loots goes on to explain that administrative law and labour law are inter-related and mutually supporting rights.59 She cites the implicit recognition of interdependence in both labour and administrative law in support of this, suggesting that it is this that allows for a purposive approach to the disclosure and consideration of social, economic, moral and political factors within the scope of public employment:60 “[T]he meaning given to one right can also be enriched by recognizing and giving appropriate expression to the inter-connectedness between that right and other fundamental rights.”61
  Finally, it is suggested that the rich administrative law jurisprudence built up in South Africa, also in relation to the public employment relationship, generally remains of importance, also for the further development of labour law and other areas of law which are relevant for the said relationship. At a constitutional level, this flows from the interdependency, interrelatedness and indivisibility of the range of fundamental rights pertaining to, in this case, the employment sphere, and more specifically the public employment context – as noted by the Constitutional Court in Gcaba v Minister of Safety and Security.62 It is on this basis that this contribution will deal with the administrative law jurisprudence that has developed over the years.
