Keywords

Mini Summary


The applicant was dismissed by the respondent from her position as administrative controller, having been charged with failing to follow the respondent's stock loss procedure. She referred a dispute to the CCMA, alleging that she had been unfairly dismissed.

Held that the onus of proving the fairness of the dismissal rests with the employer.

The applicant's challenge to the procedural fairness of the dismissal was based on the fact that she was not allowed representation by a union official of her own choice. The arbitrator pointed out that the applicant was only entitled to be represented by a union member who was also an employee of the respondent. She did not have the right to call upon a union official from outside of the company.

On the issue of substantive fairness, the arbitrator found that the respondent was correct in finding that the applicant was actually involved in the stock losses suffered by the company. The application was dismissed.

[2]    Issue(s) to be decided

The main issue in dispute relates to the alleged unfair dismissal of the applicant in terms of the Labour Relations Act 66 of 1995, section 191(1). I am called upon to decide on the fairness thereof and to render an appropriate award.
During opening statements, the applicant specifically challenged the fairness of her dismissal on the following grounds:
Procedural fairness: The applicant was denied representation by a union official (SATAWU) at the disciplinary-/appeal hearings.
Substantive fairness: The applicant denied that she committed any offence/was not guilty as to the charges at hand.

[5]    Analysis of evidence/argument

Having heard the (closing) arguments of both parties, I will address the material issues separately.

Onus of prove

 The onus to proof the fairness of a dismissal dispute rests on the employer's party/respondent (section 192(2)/Labour Relations Act 66 of 1995). The proof required is on a balance of probabilities.

Procedural fairness: Representation at disciplinary hearings
The applicant's main argument is that she was "denied her legal right to be represented by a Union Official of her own choice". Mr Mazibuko has taken this argument further, arguing that at the workplace only two employees are members of SATAWU/SACCAWU, hence it was difficult for the applicant to arrange for a shop steward or co-employee to represent her. He relied heavily on the dicta in NUMSA v Transvaal Pressed Nuts, Bolts & Rivets (Pty) Ltd (1988) 9 ILJ 457 (IC) (sic).
In terms of Schedule 8, Item 4(1)(2): 
[2007] JOL 19663 (CCMA) at 16
 (a) An employee is allowed to be assisted by a trade union representative or fellow employee at disciplinary hearings (item 4(1)); and
before disciplinary action is taken against a shop steward, the union must first be informed/consulted (item 4(2)).

  In terms of section 213 a "trade union representative” means a member of a trade union who is elected to represent employees at the workplace. A "trade union representative" in this context means an elected shop steward (either part-time or full-time) and excludes union officials and legal practitioners (being third parties to the direct employee/employer relationship). The reason is obvious: internal disciplinary proceedings must be dealt with the minimum of legal formalities.

Secondly, I feel obliged to point out that Mr Mazibuko, with respect, clearly misguided him with the dicta in NUMSA v Transvaal Pressed Nuts, Bolts & Rivets (Pty) Ltd. (1988) 9 ILJ 457 (IC). In the specific case de Villiers SC/member clearly stated that "a union has no clear right to demand representation by officials for members at disciplinary hearings unless agreed upon to the contrary" and "that a general bar on such representatives does not amount to an unfair labour practice". The learned author came to this conclusion after he had analysed the case law at hand. In final analysis he relied on MAWU v IA Bell Equipment Co (unreported) date 24 September 1986 where the following was held:

"Moreover (it was submitted) he should have been entitled to be represented by trade union officials at the enquiries conducted into both of these alleged offences. The company document headed 'disciplinary procedure for all scheduled employees with effective date 1 January 1985' was on of the exhibits. It provides that the employee has the right to be represented by an employee representative who must be a fellow employee. There is no reference to representation by a trade union official. Although it might be in appropriate circumstances be commendable industrial relations practice for an employer to permit an employee to be  
[2007] JOL 19663 (CCMA) at 17
represented by a trade union official, particularly if the employee is a shop steward or office-bearer in the union, even though the union is not formally recognised by the employer, it does not appear to the court that this is a clear right of an employee, whether office-bearer, shop steward or ordinary member of a union. It is only where a recognition agreement provides specifically for such representation that there would be a clear right in the employee to be so represented."

See also Wahl v AECI Ltd. (1983) 4 ILJ 298 (IC) read with ILO Convention: Termination of Employment Recommendation 119 of 1963. This position has not since changed.
I also cannot agree with Mr Mazibuko's contention that in the specific circumstances the applicant was one of two employees/members and could not easily have arranged for another person to represent her save for a union official (or outsider). The rules of the company are quite clear and the applicant in fact had a choice of other employees (60–70) within the group of companies at Bethlehem. In fact, the applicant/her representative was at least on four occasions advised about her rights, but nevertheless decided not to attend the hearings. In this regard I am guided by Gimini Indent Agencies CC t/a S&A Marketing v CCMA (1999) 20 ILJ 2872 (LC) where it was held that in circumstances alike, an employee cannot ex post facto claim procedural unfairness.

  –See also L Mthembu v Foshini (2006): FS 3010–06 date 11 December 2006 where a similar approach was held by Commissioner M Louw.

Substantive fairness
Re Charge one: Stock losses: Collective guilt
Firstly, I must point out that in common law there rest a general duty on  
[2007] JOL 19663 (CCMA) at 18
  employees to act with fidelity and due care towards the interest of its employer. Failure to follow suit amounts to a form of dishonesty/breach of trust which is destructive of the employment relationship. See Standard Bank of South Africa v CCMA (1998) 19 ILJ 903 (LC); County Fair Foods (Pty) Ltd v CCMA & others (1999) 20 ILJ 1701 (LAC).
These duties, bestowed upon employees by common law also include that it is incumbent on employees to (timeously) speak up and to report any irregularities that comes to his/her knowledge or, as in the present circumstances, could reasonably have been expected to come to his/her knowledge (see BMW (SA) (Pty) Ltd v Van der Walt (2000) 21 ILJ 113 (LAC)).
In fact, our legislator has now adopted legislation to protect these so-called whistle-blowers ("splitters") in terms of the Protected Disclosures Act 26 of 2000, section 3. The purpose of this legal protection also justifies a difference in approach/application of disciplinary measures in favour of the whistle-blower – if that was not the case, there would be no motivation/incentive for an employee to blow the whistle/open the can of worms (and in doing so, incriminate him-/herself). The whistle-blower is thus entitled to protection by means of indemnity. See K Grieve v Denel (Pty) Ltd (2003) case number C7/03 date 16 January 2003 (unreported) where Pillmer AJ held that the said act:

". . . seems to encourage a culture of whistle blowing and to create a culture which will facilitate the disclosure of information by employees relating to criminal and other irregular conduct in the workplace by also providing protection against any reprisals as a result of such disclosures".

  See also FEDCRAW obo Selepe & others v Snip Trading (Pty) Ltd (2003) FS 4666–02 (CCMA).

 
[2007] JOL 19663 (CCMA) at 19
In Chauke & others v Lee Service Centre CC t/a Leeson Motors (1998) 19 ILJ 1441 (LAC), the Labour Appeal Court per Cameron JA (as he then was) held that in circumstances where an employer is not in the position to identify the perpetrators, it is justifiable to hold the group of employees collectively responsible. The court based its decision on the fact that it is either a derivative violation of trust and confidence or the evidential inference of a positive involvement of a co-employee. The employees thus culpably participated in a "campaign" (or "scheme") and share the responsibility of misconduct committed.
In Council for Scientific & Industrial Research v Fijen (1996) 17 ILJ 18 (A) the Appeal Court restated the importance of the underlying common law-duties within the context of our labour law. The Court held at 26D–E as follows:

"Where a worker has or may reasonably be supposed to have information concerning the guilty, his or her failure to come forward with the information may itself amount to misconduct. The relationship between employer and employee is in its essentials one of trust and confidence, and, even at common law, conduct clearly inconsistent with that essential warranted termination of employment. Failure to assist an employer in bringing the guilty to book violates this duty and may itself justify dismissal."

And in FAWU & others v ABI Ltd (1994) 15 ILJ 1057 (LAC) the Labour Appeal Court (in re a case where a large group of workers assaulted a "scab" driver/severely injured) held per Nugent J as follows:

"In the field of industrial relations, it may be that policy considerations require more of an employee than that he merely remained passive in circumstances like the present, and that his failure to assist in an investigation of this sort may in itself justify disciplinary action".

 
[2007] JOL 19663 (CCMA) at 20
Our labour tribunals furthermore expand the above principles (relating to actions of misconduct) to actions of poor performance especially relating to stock losses/-shrinkage and/or to achieve certain performance standards in circumstances where the cause of non-achievement could not be linked to a single factor or a particular individual.
In F Mkhwanzi & 2 others/Pep Stores (Pty) Ltd KN35074/7 March 2000 Commissioner Jenlan stated that the dismissal can be justified when:
Acts/omissions constituting poor work performance have been committed by individuals in the group.
That the respondent has acted reasonably in identifying the group of applicants who could have under-performed and that each member of the group was individually capable of doing so.
That as between the members of the group the respondent could not reasonably identify the individuals causing the required standard not to be met.
The respondent has made a sufficiently thorough investigation to establish the reasons for the unsatisfactory performance of the group.
The respondent has given individual applicants in the group appropriate evaluation, instruction, training, guidance or counselling and a reasonable time to improve.
The applicants in the group were aware or could reasonably be expected to have been aware of the required group performance standard.
The group performance standard is reasonable and achievable.
The applicants were given a fair opportunity to meet the required performance standard and continue to perform unsatisfactory.
The applicants have been given the right to be heard and to be assisted.
Dismissal is an appropriate sanction for not meeting the required standard.
See also SACCAWU v Cashbuild Ltd [1996] 4 BLLR 457 (IC); SACCAWU v Pep Stores (1998) 18 ILJ 939 (CCMA); SACTWU obo Motaung & others (2001): FS 19142 (CCMA), which held a similar approach.
Lastly, I must also point out that I cannot align myself with Mr Mazibuko's contention that the applicant had no knowledge of the vast stock losses. In fact, the applicant contradicted herself on various occasions: I fail to understand how  
[2007] JOL 19663 (CCMA) at 21
  she could not have any knowledge of the increasing stock losses and/or denied that management ever discussed this burning issue with her/the staff in circumstances where:
She attended all the renewed training sessions re stock losses.
She attended staff meetings.
She received two letters to the same effect?
Re Charges two & three: Theft/failure to report theft to management
From the outset I must point out that the applicant/her representative never challenged the direct evidence of Me Meisie Jokweni where she:
Directly implicated the applicant to theft.
Pointed out that the applicant send her a SMS to "cover up the theft".
There is no doubt in my mind that the applicant, together with Meisie, Lettie and Lerato formed part of the concert/scam to steal goods at the store. Such theft usually took place when the store manager (Joyce Motaung) was not at the store, and, the applicant being the 2IC was the only supervisor present. The applicant was placed in a position of trust, which she bluntly failed to carry out.
I cannot agree with Mr Mazibuko's contention that it was unfair for the employer/respondent to only search the house of Meisie once they got the lead and/or that Meisie was forced by the police to make statements and/or that once she was caught red-handed, she decided to frame the applicant/other co-employees. Firstly, Me Meisie Jokweni was a flexi-time worker who only worked 36 hours per month – the stock losses also occurred whilst she was not at the store.
