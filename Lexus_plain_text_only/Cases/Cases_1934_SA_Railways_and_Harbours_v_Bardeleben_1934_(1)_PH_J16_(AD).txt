Held:

The appeal succeeded with costs and judgment below must be altered into one for the Defendant with costs.
Per Wessels, C.J.: The judgment below gave so vivid a picture of the accident and so careful an analysis of the evidence that the task of the Appellate Division had been considerably lightened. It was no easy task in these modern cases of collision between very fast moving traffic to reconstruct accur-ately what had happened; whereas formerly with slow moving traffic the matter concerned many seconds, to-day the Court had often to deal with seconds and split seconds.
The first question to decide was whether the engine-driver had kept a proper look-out.  The Judge below had dealt so fully with the matter that his reasons might be adopted in full. It was only necessary to add that the engine-driver's first duty was to watch the crossing itself; it was also his duty to watch the danger spot (the fig tree), but it was not his duty to keep his eye fixed on that tree merely because a careless motorist might emerge from it at an unreasonable speed. Superhuman powers must not be expected of engine-drivers; it was enough if they acted as reasonable men.  In this case the driver had kept a proper look-out.
Was it then negligence not to have whistled 15 to 20 yards away from the crossing? This point had often been considered by the Appellate Division-see Lee's case (1927 A.D. 202); Mancho's case (1928 A.D. 94);  Stegman's  case  (1932 A.D.

That no doubt was a counsel of perfection after the event but the Court must find as a fact that a reasonable man in the position of an engine-driver at this particular crossing should know that he ought to continue  his blast until he was 20 yards  from  the crossing,  notwithstanding the guidance  got  from  the  regula-tion.
