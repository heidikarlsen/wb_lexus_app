



133 Public interest privilege
The Law of South Africa (LAWSA)


LAWSA  >  The Law of South Africa (LAWSA)  >  Delict  >  Defences: Grounds Of Justification

133  Public interest privilege  Despite the important role that the media play in a properly-functioning democratic society, courts have consistently rejected any attempt to give the media wider freedom of expression than that afforded to ordinary citizens.1 In Neethling v Du Preez; Neethling v The Weekly Mail 2 the Appellate Division rejected any suggestion that public interest alone, without proving the truth of material, could constitute a defence in our law. The court a quo had accepted the view expressed in an earlier decision, Zillie v Johnson,3 that a newspaper is entitled to publish defamatory matter in circumstances where the public has a right to be informed of the facts. The Appellate Division noted that the notion of newspaper privilege is unknown in our law, and that newspapers do not occupy a special position.4 The Supreme Court of Appeal appears now to have relaxed that approach in National Media Ltd v Bogoshi,5 although it was at pains to emphasise that standards applicable to media have not been lowered or watered down and that media do not have carte blanche to publish whatever they like.6 The attitudinal change is the result of a change in focus: Neethling was concerned with the defence of truth and public benefit – for which truth is an essential requirement – while in Bogoshi the emphasis was on the publication of untrue statements. Although it did not expressly say so, the Supreme Court of Appeal seems to have viewed the issue as one of media, or public interest, privilege.7
  The Bogoshi judgment establishes the following rule: Publication in the press of false defamatory material in which the public has an interest will not be unlawful if, upon a consideration of all the circumstances of the case, it is found to have been reasonable to publish the particular facts in the particular way and at the particular time.8 The inquiry is firmly located in the concept of objective reasonableness, based upon society’s legal convictions.9 And this, it is suggested, makes public interest a crucial factor – either, in respect of the material itself, or in the act of publication.10 Other aspects to consider, amongst others,11 are the nature, extent and tone of the allegations; the nature and reliability of the information upon which the allegations were based; the steps taken to verify them;12 and the urgency or need to publish the allegations before positively establishing the truth.13
  This form of privilege, it is suggested, should be available to all defendants, not only the media. It is trite that the occasion, not the person, is privileged, and any restriction of the rule would result in what the courts have tried to avoid, namely, affording media special treatment not available to others. Surely a false statement made by an ordinary citizen – a rate-payer at a municipal meeting, for example – should be similarly treated?
  Recognition of a public interest privilege, either independently, or as an additional sub-category of the standard defence of privileged occasion, would be a welcome development. Freedom of expression now enjoys more prominence than in the past, and the role of the media in ensuring adherence to democratic values, such as openness, accountability and the right to information, is similarly enshrined.14 Media have a constitutional obligation in relation to freedom of expression. They should foster it, provide a platform for the exchange of ideas, and ensure that ordinary citizens have sufficient information to be responsible and effective members of society.15 Public interest should of course not be circumscribed, but this particular form of privileged occasion should especially be available to defendants where the defamatory material relates to public figures or refers to public duties or political or governmental activity, or where the press exercises its function to ferret out corruption, dishonesty and graft.16 or to protect genuine whistle-blowers.
  It is suggested that requirements for the defence of privileged occasion could easily be adapted to accommodate the factors mentioned in Bogoshi and that the following requirements should be present for this defence to succeed: Where defamatory material is published, a defendant may put up a defence that the material, irrespective of its truth, was published bona fide and without malice in the public interest and in circumstances in which it was reasonable to do so.17
  The defendant should then be able to rebut the presumption of unlawfulness by proving that the plaintiff participated in political activity, or was entrusted with public duties; that, in the circumstances, the defendant had a duty to inform society. Put differently: That publication of the material was in the public interest and promoted an open and democratic society; and that the material was relevant and germane to the issue. It would then still be open to the plaintiff to show malice, that the defendant had no reasonable grounds for believing the truth of the material, that no foundation for the publication existed in the circumstances,18 or that the information published in the material was obtained by unlawful means.19
  In all, the defendant should be given a privilege similar to that enjoyed by persons who report on proceedings of courts, Parliament and other public bodies, to report or comment on matters of public interest.20
  The onus should be on the defendant to prove the defence on a balance of probabilities.21
