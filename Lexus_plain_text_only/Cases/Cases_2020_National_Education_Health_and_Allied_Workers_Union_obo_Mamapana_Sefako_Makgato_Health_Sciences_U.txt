



National Education Health and Allied Workers’ Union obo Mamapana / Sefako Makgato Health Sciences University [2020] 9 BALR 941 (CCMA)
Arbitration Law Reports

Division: Commission for Conciliation, Mediation and Arbitration
Case No: GATW950/20
Date: 20/02/2020
Before: L Dekker, Commissioner
Keywords


Referral in terms of section 191(5)(a)(iv) of the LRA
Unfair labour practices — Suspension — Employee referring dispute over suspension before it was extended pending outcome of disciplinary hearing — Referral premature.

Editor’s Summary


The applicant employee was placed on precautionary suspension in terms of the respondent’s disciplinary code. Before the suspension was extended after the employee was formally charged with corruption pending the conclusion of a disciplinary hearing, the applicant union referred a dispute concerning his suspension. The respondent contended in limine that the Commission lacked jurisdiction because the employee had claimed that he was a “whistle blower”. The applicants argued that the CCMA was bound to arbitrate the matter because the dispute had been certified unresolved.

The Commissioner rejected the applicant’s submission that the CCMA had jurisdiction to arbitrate simply because the matter had been certified unresolved. Once the matter was set down for arbitration, the jurisdictional point had to be determined. The Commissioner noted further that the dispute had been referred before the suspension had been extended. His earlier suspension could accordingly not be “uplifted”, which meant that the present application was premature. The applicants’ only option was to refer a fresh dispute concerning the current suspension. 

The application was dismissed.

Ruling

The matter was then referred to me for Arbitration where the respondent’s attorney raised a point in limine that the CCMA lacks jurisdiction in the matter. 
By mutual agreement it was agreed by the parties to submit written heads of argument by 17 February 2020. The respondent was represented by

The representative on behalf of the respondent submitted pages 1 to 8 (paragraphs 1 to 4.8).

Unfair labour practice (suspension) – is this an occupational detriment in breach of section 3 of the Protected Disclosures Act (“PDA”) 26 of 2000 to be adjudicated by the Labour Court in terms of the proviso to sect ion 4(2)(b) of the PDA.

Submissions obo respondent
The following submissions were made on behalf of the respondent in its written submissions regarding the nature of the dispute [numbering retained]:

“3.  NATURE OF DISPUTE

3.1 It is evident from the Applicant’s submissions on record that the real issue in dispute according to him, is that he was suspended by the management of the University to silence him as a whistle blower. The allegation is clear that he was subjected to an occupational detriment (suspension) on account, or partly on account, of him having made a protected disclosure (whistleblower about alleged maladministration and misappropriation). The Applicant in no unclear terms alleges that: ‘The suspension of Mr TR Mampana was a tactic by certain members of MANCO to silence our member as a whistle blower to the Union and has ulterior motives’”.
The conclusion of the respondent is set out in paragraph 4 as follows [numbering retained]:
“4.1 Rule 22 of the CCMA regulates jurisdictional points and states the following: 

‘If during the arbitration proceedings it appears that a jurisdictional issue has not been determined, the commissioner must require the referring party to prove that the Commission has jurisdiction to arbitrate the dispute.’

4.2 The CCMA is a creature of statute and is not a court of law. As a general rule, it cannot finally decide its own jurisdiction. The general principle is that the commissioner is entitled to assume provisional jurisdiction, but may decline jurisdiction if it becomes apparent that the actual issue in dispute is one over which the CCMA lacks jurisdiction.
4.3 Jurisdiction is determined based on the existence of objective facts. It is evident from the submissions of the Applicant on record that he alleges that his suspension by the University was for an ulterior motive. The Applicant’s case touches on all the elements required by the PDA. The Applicant makes allegations that he is subjected to an occupational detriment (suspension) because of him reporting an act of impropriety by the University. The Applicant alleges that because of his whistle blowing of the alleged ‘Maladministration and Misappropriation’, his suspension is a ‘tactic used by certain members of MANCO [Management Committee of the University] to silence’ the Applicant as a ‘whistle blower’. 
4.4 When a Commissioner considers the objective facts with a view to determine the question of jurisdiction, it is not required to determine the strength of the allegations or pronounce on the prospects of success. The Commissioner is only tasked to determine which forum should adjudicate that dispute.
4.5 In the event that the Applicant in his written submissions attempts to backpedal regarding the real reason for his suspension, and/or make new submissions in an attempt to clothe the CCMA with jurisdiction, these submissions should be viewed with skepticism. A party cannot 

for convenience contend that the dispute relates to a ‘normal unfair labour practice’ merely in order to bring the issue into the ambit of the CCMA’s arbitration jurisdiction. A commissioner is still obliged to determine the objective facts to decide the jurisdiction of the CCMA. 

4.6 It further bears mentioning that Section 3 of the PDA applies even if the occupational detriment is only partly on account of having made a protected disclosure. Therefore, even if the allegation is that the occupational detriment only partly as a result of the protective disclosure and other aspect of the unfair labour practice is challenged, then still, the Labour Court should adjudicate the whole dispute.
4.7 In the case of Medi-Clinic Bpk v Fick & others the Labour Court noted that a commissioner is obliged to determine the objective facts in a case and decide whether the matter should be arbitrated or adjudicated in the Labour Court. Jurisdiction does not rest on matters of convenience.
4.8 It is submitted that considering the objective facts, the dispute should be adjudicated by the Labour Court as contemplated by Section 4(2)(b) of the PDA”.

Evaluation and conclusion by Commissioner 

Chronically, the events relating to this matter are as follows.

First suspension 
In a letter dated 30 July 2019 the applicant was placed under Precautionary Suspension up to 30 October 2019 with full pay in terms of Clause 7.1 of the Disciplinary Code of the University (pages R12/13).

Second suspension 
In a letter dated 2 December 2019 the Precautionary Suspension was extended from 30 October 2019 for three [3] months to 30 January 2020, after the initial suspension was reviewed in terms of clause 7.8 of the Disciplinary Code (page R14).

Third suspension
In a letter dated 30 January 2020 in terms of clause 7.10 of the Disciplinary Code the suspension was further extended beyond 30 January 2020 until the conclusion of the applicant’s disciplinary hearing, based on the following exceptional circumstances [pages R15/16]:
The seriousness of charges levelled
A charge sheet has been served on you [applicant]
Consolidation of evidence in preparation for the disciplinary hearing is at a sensitive stage”. 
Currently after 30 January 2020 the applicant is suspended in terms of the letter of 30 January 2020 (“third suspension”) until the disciplinary hearing is concluded. The applicant was also served with a charge sheet alleging that the applicant was involved in the corrupt payment of a supplier.  
[2020] 9 BALR 941 (CCMA) at 945
The dispute was referred to the CCMA (LRA7.11) on 13 January 2020, signed on 10 January 2020 and referring to a dispute that allegedly also arose on 10 January 2020 (LRA7.11, paragraph 5). No dispute arose on that date.
NEHAWU on 7 February 2020 wrote a letter on behalf of the applicant to the Council and Executive Management of the respondent University with the subject (page A72): “Whistleblowing of Maladministration and Misappropriations in the University by Mr TR Mampana”. It referred to the following five [5] examples where the applicant allegedly acted as a “Whistle-blower” in terms of the Protected Disclosures Act (“PDA”):
“1. Neotel Network telephone infrastructure
2. DBSA managed projects
3. Sports indaba consultation process
4. PSP Icon appointment
5. Japan sponsored trip”

  The last paragraph of the NEHAWU letter reads:

“. . . the suspension of Mr Mampana was a tactic used by certain members of MANCO to silence our member as a whistle blower to the Union and has ulterior motives”. 


Based on these allegations by NEHAWU in its letter to the University the respondent raised the point in limine that in terms of section 186(2)(d) of the Labour Relations Act (“LRA”) read with section 4(2)(b) of the PDA, the true nature of the dispute relates to an occupational detriment (suspension) of the employee based on the fact that he was a “whistle-blower” and the CCMA lacks jurisdiction to attend to the dispute relating to an “occupational detriment”.

Premature referral 
It is not necessary for me to make a ruling on the legal issue raised by the respondent as the 13 January 2020 referral of the dispute to the CCMA by the Union on behalf of the applicant is fatally flawed – it is premature and was referred before the current Third Suspension of the applicant as from 30 January 2020 until the conclusion of the disciplinary hearing. The Second Suspension lapsed on 30 January 2020 and cannot legally be “uplifted”. The third suspension only took effect on 30 January 2020, seventeen [17] days after the dispute was prematurely referred to the CCMA. 
It would be legally possible for the applicant and his Union to submit a de novo new referral of a dispute in respect of an alleged “unfair labour practice” (suspension) to the CCMA in terms of section 191(1)(b)(ii) of the LRA within ninety [90] days of the occurrence of the third suspension on 30 January 2020 which is the only suspension that currently legally exists. 

Effect of certificate
The applicant’s representative submitted in paragraphs 6 and 7 the written submission as follows [numbering retained]:
“[6] Following the con/ arb that was held before Commissioner Makgobela on 10 February 2020, the dispute between the Applicant and the Respondent remained unresolved. The result was that a certificate of 

outcome was then issued by Commissioner Makgobela. For ease of reference, a copy of the certificate of outcome is attached hereto and marked “RMI”.

[7] It is trite law and we do not want to burden the Commissioner with endless authorities that confirm that a certificate of outcome, once issued, amounts to a licence to arbitrate. The Commissioner now has a licence to arbitrate. The question then is what dispute to arbitrate?”
This is not correct. The certificate does NOT confer jurisdiction. It only enables a dispute to be set down for arbitration where the issue of jurisdiction may be determined see “City of Ekurhuleni Metropolitan Municipality and SALGBC (JR246/17) [2018] ZALCJHB 300 [2018] at paragraph 10”.

Ruling 
The 13 January 2020 referral of the dispute by the union/applicant regarding an alleged “unfair labour practice” was premature as the previous second suspension lapsed on 30 January 2020 and the current third suspension only took effect on 30 January 2020 and is in place until the conclusion of the disciplinary hearing.
The fatally flawed premature application of 13 January 2020 is dismissed. 
The applicant can consider submitting a new dispute to the CCMA in respect of the 30 January 2020 suspension.
