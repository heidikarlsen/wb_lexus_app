



266 Exclusive jurisdiction of the Labour Court
The Law of South Africa (LAWSA)


LAWSA  >  The Law of South Africa (LAWSA)  >  Labour Law: Part 2  >  Resolution Of Labour Disputes  >  Adjudication Of Labour Disputes  >  Labour Court  >  Jurisdiction

266  Exclusive jurisdiction of the Labour Court  Except where an Act of Parliament provides otherwise, the Labour Court has exclusive jurisdiction in respect of all matters provided for in the Labour Relations Act 1 or where any other Act of Parliament 2 gives jurisdiction to the Labour Court. In matters concerning the following, the Labour Court has exclusive jurisdiction:
(a) the exercise of rights of freedom of association;3
(b) freedom of association and victimisation;4
(c) aspects concerning the administration of contributions for closed shop and agency shop agreements that may be appealed to the Labour Court;5
(d) the admission of a registered trade union to a closed shop agreement;6
(e) the admission of a party to a bargaining council;7
  (f )   the winding-up and cancellation of the registration of a bargaining council;8
(g) the interpretation or application of bargaining council determinations;9
(h) whether a secondary strike is reasonable in relation to its impact on the primary employer;10
(i) an application to recover the value of payment in kind made by an employer at the request of striking or locked-out employees;11
  ( j)   interdicts and orders in respect of conduct in contemplation or furtherance of strikes and lock-outs or for equitable compensation in respect of strikes or lock-outs not in compliance with the Labour Relations Act;12
(k) a breach of picketing rules;13
(l) interdicts and declaratory orders in respect of socio-economic protest action;14
(m) an application by a trustee for an order declaring that the implementation of an arbitration award made in respect of changes by the employer or its representative in trusts or boards of employer-controlled schemes or social benefit schemes constitutes a breach of a fiduciary duty;15
(n) the winding-up of trade unions and employers’ organisations because they do not comply with the Act, or on insolvency or cancellation of their registration;16
(o) the review of arbitration awards of the Commission for Conciliation, Mediation and Arbitration (CCMA);17
(p) the non-compliance with a trade union’s or employers’ organisation’s constitution;18
(q) automatically unfair dismissals, dismissals for operational reasons and dismissals flowing from strikes or closed shop agreements;19
(r) unfair labour practice disputes concerning discrimination; and
(s) if an employee is dismissed by reason of the employer’s operational requirements only affecting that single employee, such an employee may elect to refer the dispute either to arbitration or to the Labour Court;20
(t) appeals against decisions of the Registrar of Labour;21
(u) appeals against compliance orders issued by the Director-General of the Department of Labour;22
(v) make a compliance order issued by the Director-General of the Department of Labour an order of court;23
(w) review the performance or purported performance of any function provided for in the Basic Conditions of Employment Act or any act or omission by any person or body in terms of that Act on any grounds permissible in law;24
(x) make a reasonable determination on any matter concerning a contract of employment that could include an order for specific performance, damages or compensation;25
(y) disputes about the interpretation or application of the Employment Equity Act,26 except where that Act provides otherwise;27
(z) appeals against decisions of the Chief Inspector in terms of the Occupational Health and Safety Act;27
(aa) all matters arising from the Skills Development Act,28 except where the Employment Equity Act 29 provides otherwise.30

1	66 of 1995: s 157(1). See also par 323 post. The issue of jurisdiction has to be determined on the basis of the pleadings and not the merits of the case: Chirwa v Transnet 2008 2 BLLR 97 (CC); 2008 3 BCLR 251 (CC) 2008 ILJ 73 (CC) par 169 and Mahumani v MEC: Finance, Economic Affairs & Tourism, Limpopo 2010 ILJ 2009 (NGP). It may well be that, after hearing the facts, the Labour Court may decide that it does not have jurisdiction: Wardlaw v Supreme Mouldings 2007 ILJ 1042 (LAC) par 24. See also Goldfields Mining SA v CCMA 2010 ILJ 371 (LC) and Goussard v Impala Platinum 2012 ILJ 2898 (LC). The Labour Court also has jurisdiction in terms of the Defence Special Tribunal Act 81 of 1998, Skills Development Act 97 of 1998, Skills Development Levies Act 9 of 1999, Occupational Health and Safety Act 85 of 1993, Mine Health and Safety Act 29 of 1996, Promotion of Access to Information Act 2 of 2000 and the Protected Disclosures Act 26 of 2000, the detail of which falls outside the scope of this work. See Landman and Van Niekerk Practice in the Labour Courts A-9. In Mgijima v Eastern Cape Appropriate Technology Unit 2000 ILJ 291 (Tk) 302 Van Zyl J states: “Accordingly, a dispute about an unfair dismissal is a ‘matter’ as envisaged by s 157(1) of the Act. Further, applying a purposive approach and reading s 157(1) in the light of the objects of the Act, I agree with the conclusion reached by Van Dijkhorst J in IMATU v Northern Pretoria Metropolitan Substructure 1999 ILJ 1018 (T), namely that it was the clear intention of the legislature to create specialised fora to deal with labour related matters wherever the Act provides for specific dispute resolution procedures.” The Labour Court does not have jurisdiction in respect of a dispute concerning the eviction of employees from the property they live on as part of their contract of service: Natal Trust Farms v Nzukulu 1999 ILJ 78 (N). In the following cases, the Labour Court found that it has exclusive jurisdiction: Adonis v Western Cape Education Department 1997 8 BLLR 1005 (LC); Mondi Paper v PPWAWU 1997 ILJ 84 (D); Coin Security Group v SANUSOW 1998 ILJ 43 (C); Sappi Fine Papers v PPWAWU 1998 ILJ 246 (SE). In Kilpert v Buitendach 1997 ILJ 1296 (W) the court found that the jurisdiction of the High Court was not ousted in a dispute concerning the termination of the contract of employment. See also Fabricius 1998 ILJ 431. In NTU v Superintendent General: Department of Education & Culture, KwaZulu-Natal 2008 ILJ 1727 (LC) the court found that it has jurisdiction to hear an urgent application where an employer has failed to provide information, although the Labour Relations Act 66 of 1995 s 16 provides that a dispute concerning the provision of information should be arbitrated. (In this case, the application was not successful for various other reasons). In SAMWU v eThekwini Municipality 2008 ILJ 765 (LC) pars 21 35 the court found that, if it could issue a declarator and an interdict, the court had jurisdiction to hear a dispute concerning the interpretation of a collective agreement that in terms of s 24 was reserved for arbitration. (The court in all probability came to the right conclusion, however, based on untenable reasons.) In Booysen v Minister of Safety & Security 2011 ILJ 112 (LAC) 129 the court referred to various cases dealing with jurisdiction and found that it had jurisdiction to interdict any unfair conduct including to decide whether suspension without pay, pending a disciplinary hearing, was fair. In Volkswagen SA v Koorts 2011 ILJ 1892 (LAC) the court found that it had jurisdiction to decide the validity of a private arbitration agreement and to review the arbitrator’s findings. However, in Telcordia Technologies Inc v Telkom SA 2007 2 All SA 243 (SCA); 2007 5 BCLR 503 (SCA); 2007 3 SA 266 (SCA) 292 par 51 which was confirmed in Lufuno Mphaphuli & Associates v Andrews 2009 6 BCLR 527 (CC); 2009 4 SA 529 (CC) the grounds for review were limited to those in the Arbitration Act 42 of 1965 s 33. In Meyer v Horizon Carpet Manufacturers CC 2011 ILJ 1939 (LC) it was found that the employee’s claim, in so far as the Close Corporations Act 69 of 1984 was concerned, was incidental to the main claim, a claim which did fall within the jurisdiction of the Labour Court. The employee would have to bring a separate claim in the provincial division of the High Court in terms of the Close Corporations Act if the Labour Court did not hear this claim with the main claim. This, the court found, would lead to an unnecessary duplication of costs and delays the legislature could not have intended. It would not be in accordance with the purpose of the Labour Relations Act 66 of 1995, ie the effective resolution of labour disputes, nor with the object of the Basic Conditions of Employment Act 75 of 1997 to give effect to the right to fair labour practices conferred by the Constitution of the Republic of SA, 108 of 1996 s 23(1). If the Labour Court did not have jurisdiction, the parties could not through agreement “give the Labour Court jurisdiction”: Mohale v Net 1 Applied 2013 ILJ 930 (LC).

19	S 191(5)(b) read with s 191(11)(a). S 191(5) reads: “If a council or a commissioner has certified that the dispute remains unresolved, or, if 30 days have expired since the council or the Commission received the referral and the dispute remains unresolved . . .” S 135(2) provides that the CCMA must attempt to resolve a dispute within 30 days after the receipt of a referral. At the end of the 30 days or its extension, the CCMA must issue a certificate that the dispute was not resolved if that was the case: s 135(5). It follows that, if the CCMA did not attempt to resolve the dispute within 30 days (because it is obligatory), the commissioner does not have the jurisdiction to issue a certificate and, if the commissioner issues a certificate, such certificate would be invalid and the Labour Court would not have jurisdiction in terms of s 191(5). The Labour Appeal Court rejected this argument in Fidelity Guards Holdings v Epstein 2000 ILJ 2382 (LAC). It is suggested that the Labour Court would in any event have jurisdiction to hear the matter, because s 191(5) reads “or, if the 30 days have expired”. This is therefore an alternative ground for jurisdiction. The 90-day period runs from the date of certification and not after the 30-day period has expired. See NUM v Hermic Exploration 2003 ILJ 787 (LAC) 802 and Sappi Timber Industries v CCMA 2003 ILJ 846 (LC). The court would be reluctant to deny a person access to the court due to a fault by a government body such as the CCMA, therefore a certificate not issued within a reasonable time is not a nullity. The “penalty” an applicant suffers is an appropriate award, and not denial of access to the court. The fact that a certificate has been issued does not preclude the Labour Court to consider whether the CCMA had jurisdiction to hear the matter in the first instance: AVBOB Mutual Assurance Society v CCMA Bloemfontein 2003 ILJ 535 (LC). If the Labour Court’s jurisdiction is challenged, the court will consider the matter in order to establish whether in fact it has jurisdiction or not. See NUMSA v Driveline Technologies 2000 ILJ 142 (LAC); Wardlaw v Supreme Moulding supra. The Labour Court would not hear unfair dismissal cases in terms of s 191(5) even if the certificate of outcome indicates that the matter should be heard by the Labour Court. The commissioner’s categorisation of the dispute and his view and indication of the appropriate forum for adjudication is not binding on any party to the dispute and has no legal or jurisdictional consequence: Pienaar v Stellenbosch University 2012 ILJ 2445 (LC).
20	S 191(12). In Bracks v Rand Water 2010 ILJ 897 (LAC) the court found that an individual had the choice to either approach the CCMA or the Labour Court when he or she had been dismissed for operational reasons and that employee contended that he or she had been dismissed without due process or for substantive reasons. S 191(12) does not limit the individual’s choice of forum in the event of procedural fairness.
